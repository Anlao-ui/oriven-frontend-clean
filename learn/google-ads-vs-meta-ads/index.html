Google Ads vs Meta Ads: key differences and when to use each

Short answer

Google Ads is strongest at capturing existing demand: people already searching for what you sell. Meta Ads (Facebook and Instagram) is strongest at creating demand: showing your product to people likely to want it before they search. Most growing businesses use both, for different jobs.

The core difference: intent vs discovery

On Google Search, the user tells you what they want by typing a query. Your ad answers it. That’s why search campaigns often convert well for problems people actively look up, such as “emergency plumber” or “running shoes for flat feet”.

On Facebook and Instagram, people are scrolling, not searching. Meta’s systems decide who sees your ad based on audience signals and how people engage. That makes Meta good at introducing products people didn’t know to look for, and at reaching them visually.

How each platform decides what to show

Both run an auction, and on neither is the highest bid enough to win.

On Google, Ad Rank decides whether your ad is eligible and where it appears, based on your bid, your ad quality, the competition and the context of the search. The Quality Score you see per keyword, from 1 to 10, combines expected click-through rate, ad relevance and landing page experience. Google is clear that it’s a diagnostic and not an input in the auction, so treat it as a hint about what to improve rather than a number to chase.

On Meta, the ad with the highest total value wins, which combines your bid, how likely the person is to take the action you want, and ad quality. Your target audience decides which auctions you can enter, so creative does much of the work that keywords do on Google: it has to make the right people stop.

What that means in practice: on Google, most of your effort goes into choosing the right searches and matching them with a relevant ad and page. On Meta, it goes into producing several strong creatives and giving the system enough results to learn from.

A worked example with numbers

Suppose you sell a €60 product and have €1,000 a month to spend. These figures are made up to show the arithmetic; they aren’t benchmarks. Your own costs depend on your industry, country and offer, so replace them with your numbers.

Clicks for €1,000About 6661,000
Assumed conversion rate4%1.5%
SalesAbout 2715
Cost per saleAbout €38About €67

Three things stand out. Meta’s clicks are cheaper here, yet each sale costs more, which is why comparing click prices misleads. At a €60 price, the Meta campaign loses money on the first sale, and only makes sense if customers buy again or if it feeds later searches. And the Google result has a ceiling you can’t buy past: once you’re showing for everyone searching those terms, more budget won’t create more searches.

Budget size matters too. Meta says an ad set usually leaves its learning phase after about 50 results in the week after its last significant edit. At about €67 per sale, that’s roughly €3,350 a week. A €1,000 monthly budget is about €230 a week, or three to four sales, so an ad set optimizing for purchases may stay “Learning limited”. With a budget that size, you could use fewer ad sets, optimize for a more frequent event earlier in the funnel, or start on Google.

When to start with Google Ads

  • People already search for your product or the problem it solves.
  • You sell a service with urgent, local or high-intent demand.
  • You want traffic from people comparing options right now.

When to start with Meta Ads

  • Your product is new, visual or impulse-friendly, and few people search for it yet.
  • You can describe a clear audience by interests or demographics.
  • You have, or can make, strong images and short videos.

Situations that change the answer

  • Local services. If people search “electrician near me” when something breaks, Search usually comes first. There’s little reason to create demand for an emergency.
  • A new kind of product. If nobody knows to search for it yet, Search has little to show on. You can try related problems as keywords, but Meta can reach people who have never heard of the category.
  • B2B and high-priced services. A sale may take weeks and happen offline, so neither platform sees it unless you send that data back. Optimize for a qualified lead, not just any form fill, and judge results against your own sales records.
  • Few or no images and video. Google Search can run on text alone. Meta depends on creative, so without it you’d be starting at a disadvantage.
  • Regulated offers. On Meta, ads for employment, housing and financial products and services may require a Special Ad Category, which limits your audience options. Meta also says advertisers can’t create or edit ads about social issues, elections or politics in the EU. Check each platform’s current policy before you plan around it.

What you need to prepare for each

  • Google Search: a keyword list based on what people really type, and a responsive search ad with up to 15 headlines of 30 characters and 4 descriptions of 90 characters. Send each ad group to a page that answers that search.
  • Meta: several images or short videos in the shapes you plan to use, including vertical formats for Stories and Reels. Meta recommends a 27-character headline and 50–150 characters of primary text for Feed image ads. The Meta Pixel, ideally with the Conversions API, needs to be in place if you’re optimizing for sales or leads.
  • Both: working conversion tracking before you judge anything, and a page that delivers what the ad promised. Testing your creatives works much the same way on each.

Why many businesses run both

The platforms often work as two halves of one funnel: Meta introduces the product, and Google captures people when they later search for it or for your brand. Running both does make measurement harder. Each platform attributes conversions with its own rules, so the same sale can appear in both reports: Google Ads uses data-driven attribution by default for most conversion actions, while Meta counts actions that happen within a set number of days after someone viewed or clicked an ad. Compare each platform’s results against its own history and your actual sales, rather than adding their conversion numbers together.

Frequently asked questions

Is Google Ads or Meta Ads cheaper?

Neither is cheaper in general. Costs depend on your industry, competition, targeting and how well your ads and landing pages convert. Compare cost per acquisition for your own business, not average click prices.

Should a small business run Google Ads and Meta Ads at the same time?

With a small budget, starting with one platform that fits your demand (search or discovery) and adding the second later is often clearer. Running both from the start works when you can fund enough spend on each to learn.

Can I compare conversions across Google and Meta directly?

Only with care. Each platform uses its own attribution, so the same conversion can be counted in both. Compare trends per platform and check against your own sales data.
